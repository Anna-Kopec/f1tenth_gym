Detailed Description

Vehicle dynamics for the F1TENTH gym: single-track (bicycle) models, input constraints, and the low-level speed / steering controller.

Follows the CommonRoad single-track vehicle models. Original implementation: https://gitlab.lrz.de/tum-cps/commonroad-vehicle-models/

Models in this file

  • Kinematic single-track (KS), vehicle_dynamics_ks(): a 5-state bicycle model with no tyre slip. The wheels go exactly where they point. Accurate at low speed, wrong when cornering hard.
  • Dynamic single-track (ST), vehicle_dynamics_st(): a 7-state bicycle model with a linear tyre model, slip angle and yaw dynamics, including load transfer from longitudinal acceleration. Below 0.5 m/s it switches to the KS equations, because the ST equations divide by the speed.

Both functions return the state derivative f(x, u). They do not step the state themselves; an integrator (Euler or RK4 in base_classes.RaceCar.update_pose) does that.

Control inputs

The models take u = [steering angle velocity, longitudinal acceleration]. The gym's users command a desired steering angle and speed instead, so pid() converts between the two. Every model call first passes u through steering_constraint() and accl_constraints() to enforce the physical limits.

Clamp a requested longitudinal acceleration to what the vehicle can do.

Three limits are applied:

  • Power limit: above v_switch, the maximum forward acceleration falls off as a_max * v_switch / vel. This models a motor with constant power, where force (and so acceleration) is proportional to 1/speed.
  • Speed limits: once the car is at v_min it cannot accelerate further negative, and once at v_max it cannot accelerate further positive.
  • Braking limit: deceleration is capped at -a_max.

Low-level controller: desired (speed, steering angle) -> (acceleration, steering velocity).

This is what lets the gym take "go this fast, steer this much" as its action.

Steering (bang-bang): if the steering angle is off target by more than 1e-4 rad, steer toward the target at full rate (+/- max_sv). Otherwise hold still. The angle can overshoot by up to max_sv * dt in one step.

Speed (proportional only, no integral or derivative term): acceleration = kp * (target speed - current speed). The gain kp depends on the situation:

Current speed Speed error Situation kp
forward (v > 0) positive speeding up 10 * max_a / max_v
forward (v > 0) zero or negative braking 10 * max_a / (-min_v)
v <= 0 positive braking 2 * max_a / max_v
v <= 0 zero or negative speeding up 2 * max_a / (-min_v)

A car at a standstill (v == 0) uses the v <= 0 rows, so pulling away from rest uses the gentler gain of 2 rather than 10.

Outputs are not clamped here. accl_constraints() and steering_constraint() clamp them inside the dynamics model.

Kinematic single-track (bicycle) model: state derivative f(x, u).

It assumes the tyres roll without slipping, so the rear axle always moves along the car's heading and the yaw rate is set purely by geometry: 

x_dot     = v * cos(psi)
y_dot     = v * sin(psi)
delta_dot = u[0]  (steering velocity)
v_dot     = u[1]  (acceleration)
psi_dot   = v / (lf + lr) * tan(delta)

Here (x, y) is the rear-axle position.

Parameters
xnp.ndarray of shape (5,): state [x, y, delta, v, psi]
  • x[0]: x position in global coordinates (m)
  • x[1]: y position in global coordinates (m)
  • x[2]: front-wheel steering angle delta (rad)
  • x[3]: longitudinal velocity v (m/s)
  • x[4]: yaw angle psi (rad)
u_initnp.ndarray of shape (2,): requested input [steering velocity (rad/s), longitudinal acceleration (m/s^2)]. It is clamped by steering_constraint() and accl_constraints() before use.

Dynamic single-track (bicycle) model with tyre slip: state derivative f(x, u).

Above 0.5 m/s (the dynamic model):

  • Position moves along the velocity direction, psi + beta, not the heading psi. The car can drift sideways.
  • Each axle's lateral tyre force is linear in its slip angle. The force is scaled by mu, by that axle's cornering stiffness (C_Sf or C_Sr) and by the axle's normal load.
  • Normal loads include longitudinal load transfer. The front axle carries m*(g*lr - a*h)/(lf + lr) and the rear axle m*(g*lf + a*h)/(lf + lr), so accelerating (a > 0) shifts load to the rear and braking shifts it to the front.
  • The yaw acceleration (x[5] derivative) and slip angle rate (x[6] derivative) come from the lateral force and moment balance on these tyre forces.

Below 0.5 m/s (kinematic fallback): the dynamic equations divide by v and v^2, so they blow up near standstill. The KS model is used instead for the first five states, and:

  • the yaw rate derivative is the time derivative of the KS yaw rate, d/dt [ v / lwb * tan(delta) ] = a/lwb * tan(delta) + v/(lwb * cos^2(delta)) * delta_dot.
  • the slip angle derivative is 0, so the slip angle is held constant.
Parameters
xnp.ndarray of shape (7,): state [x, y, delta, v, psi, psi_dot, beta]
  • x[0]: x position in global coordinates (m)
  • x[1]: y position in global coordinates (m)
  • x[2]: front-wheel steering angle delta (rad)
  • x[3]: longitudinal velocity v (m/s)
  • x[4]: yaw angle psi (rad)
  • x[5]: yaw rate psi_dot (rad/s)
  • x[6]: slip angle beta at the centre of gravity (rad)

Note
(x, y) is the centre of gravity in the dynamic branch, but the KS fallback treats it as the rear axle. The difference is ignored when switching between them.
